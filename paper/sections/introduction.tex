\section{Introduction}

We study federated elections in which authorized local authorities conduct
secret local voting and compute local tallies off-chain. Ballot secrecy is
already enforced locally and local committees are assumed to count correctly.
The weak point is the transition from local results to the global result. If a
central coordinator collects local tallies and announces the winner, that
actor can omit unfavorable tallies, misreport the aggregate, or exploit early
knowledge of partial results.

The obvious countermeasure is to publish the local tallies on a blockchain and
let the final sum be computed from that public bulletin board. This fixes the
inclusion problem, but it creates a second one. Once a local tally becomes
public, the corresponding authority can be exposed to retaliation,
reputational pressure, or strategic bargaining. The problem is especially
acute in small electorates, where even an aggregate can leak sensitive local
political information. A commit-and-reveal design is better, but not
sufficient. It only postpones the disclosure of the local tally, and still
leaves a final stage in which an authority may refuse to reveal after learning
enough from the others.

This motivates the central design choice of this paper: \emph{the local tally
should remain encrypted throughout the protocol}. Each authority should publish a ciphertext
vector that represents its local tally, together with a proof that the
ciphertext hides a tally that is structurally valid. In our protocol, the
authority proves in zero knowledge that each encrypted count is in range and
that the encrypted vector sums to the public electorate size of that
authority. The contract accepts only one such ciphertext vector per authority
and fixes the complete transcript on-chain. The aggregate is then computed
homomorphically from the accepted ciphertexts, and only the global final tally
is decrypted.

Our design is closer to open-audit bulletin board systems such as
Helios~\cite{USENIX:Adida08} than to a ballot-level remote voting stack, but
it adds three protocol elements that are essential in the private-local-tally
setting: a proof that each encrypted local tally is structurally valid, an
on-chain aggregate that is updated from accepted submissions instead of posted
by a privileged actor, and a proof-checked threshold opening transcript that
reveals only the final aggregate.

We use curve-based additive ElGamal rather than Paillier~\cite{EC:Paillier99}
because the artifact is proof-heavy and therefore benefits from aligning encryption with
Circom/Groth16 arithmetic. This keeps the zero-knowledge pipeline
substantially simpler than a Paillier-based alternative. We also remain at the
local-tally level because the problem addressed here is final aggregation from
trusted local counts rather than ballot-level remote voting.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it specifies a protocol for
federated elections with encrypted local tallies, signed submission binding,
proof-based acceptance, on-chain homomorphic aggregation, and threshold final
opening. Second, it clarifies the attack surface created by visible local
tallies and shows how encrypted submissions and aggregate-only decryption
address omission, observation, retaliation, and malformed-input attacks.
Third, it provides a reproducible artifact that exercises the full workflow
end to end: trustee-set setup, encrypted tally submission, on-chain
aggregation, on-chain validation of partial decryptions, and final tally
recovery. The protocol remains narrower than a full remote voting system, but
stronger than a cleartext local-tally bulletin board.
