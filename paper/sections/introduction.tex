\section{Introduction}

We study federated elections in which authorized local authorities conduct
secret local voting and compute local tallies off-chain. Ballot secrecy is
already enforced locally and local committees are assumed to count correctly.
The weak point is the transition from local results to the global result: a
central coordinator can omit unfavorable tallies, misreport the aggregate, or
exploit early knowledge of partial results.

Publishing local tallies on a blockchain fixes inclusion but exposes local
outcomes, which can create retaliation, reputational pressure, or strategic
bargaining, especially in small electorates. Commit-and-reveal only postpones
that disclosure and still leaves a final-stage abort surface.

This motivates the central design choice of this paper: \emph{the local tally
should remain encrypted throughout the protocol}. Each authority should publish a ciphertext
vector that represents its local tally, together with a proof that the
ciphertext hides a tally that is structurally valid. In our protocol, the
authority proves in zero knowledge that each encrypted count is in range and
that the encrypted vector sums to the public electorate size of that
authority. The contract accepts only one such ciphertext vector per authority
and fixes the complete transcript on-chain. The aggregate is then computed
homomorphically from the accepted ciphertexts, and only the global final tally
is decrypted.

Our design is closer to open-audit bulletin board systems such as
Helios~\cite{USENIX:Adida08} and to multi-authority election
protocols~\cite{EC:CraGenSch97} than to a ballot-level remote voting stack, but
it adds three protocol elements that are essential in the private-local-tally
setting: a proof that each encrypted local tally is structurally valid, an
on-chain aggregate that is updated from accepted submissions instead of posted
by a privileged actor, and a proof-checked threshold opening transcript that
reveals only the final aggregate.

We use curve-based additive ElGamal rather than Paillier~\cite{EC:Paillier99}
because the artifact is proof-heavy and therefore benefits from aligning encryption with
Circom/Groth16 arithmetic. This keeps the zero-knowledge pipeline
substantially simpler than a Paillier-based alternative. We also remain at the
local-tally level because the problem addressed here is final aggregation from
trusted local counts rather than ballot-level remote voting.

The paper specifies a protocol with encrypted local tallies, signed submission
binding, proof-based acceptance, on-chain homomorphic aggregation, and
threshold final opening. It also provides a reproducible artifact that
exercises trustee setup, encrypted tally submission, on-chain aggregation,
validation of partial decryptions, and final tally recovery. The protocol
remains narrower than a full remote voting system, but stronger than a
cleartext local-tally bulletin board.
